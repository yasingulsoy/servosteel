There is no price list in this article, and there cannot be one for a roll forming line. What sets the price is not the name of the machine but the **profile you intend to make**. The same words, "trapezoidal sheet machine", can describe two lines that are far apart in cost, depending on the depth of the section and the number of bends in it.

The eight items below explain where the difference between quotations comes from. At the end there are the costs a quotation usually leaves out, and a way to put competing offers on the same footing.

## 1. The section and the number of stations

Roll forming does not bend the sheet in one go; it bends it **step by step**. Each step is a station, and each station means a pair of rolls, a shaft, bearings and a share of the drive.

A simple U section is finished in a few stations. A deep channel, a return bend or an interlocking profile needs many more. Across our lines the station count runs from **12 to 30** depending on the profile, and that range is the single biggest step in the price.

The practical rule: send the drawing and let engineering give the station count. A number quoted without the drawing is a guess.

## 2. Sheet thickness and material

As thickness rises so does forming force, and force sets shaft diameter, bearings, frame thickness and motor power. A 0.3 mm façade sheet and a 5 mm rack upright do not belong on the same line.

Material matters as much as thickness: high strength steel needs more force at the same thickness, stainless needs different roll surfaces to protect the finish, and galvanised sheet needs pressure set so the coating survives forming.

When you ask for a quotation, give the **thickness range and the material together**. "2 mm" on its own is incomplete.

## 3. Profile width

Width increases the span of the shaft. As the span grows the shaft deflects, and the way to stop deflection is a larger diameter, which means more material and more machining through every station.

So buying a line wider than you need costs more than buying one faster than you need. Take your widest profile today, add a margin, and stop there.

## 4. Is there punching in the line?

Mounting holes, cable openings, drainage slots: they are punched either in the line or afterwards on a press. In the line means a **servo punch station, a die and a measuring system**, with the feed controlled separately so hole position holds along the length.

That is the item that raises the price, and its alternative is a second machine and a second operator. Decide it on how much of your work is actually perforated.

## 5. The cut-off

Three options, three prices:

- **Stop-and-cut:** the line stops, cuts, accelerates. The most economical; it lengthens cycle time.
- **Flying shear:** cuts at line speed. Expensive mechanically and in the controls, and it pays for itself at high volume.
- **Die cut-off:** a shaped cut matching the section. The die is a separate investment.

## 6. Roll tooling: one line, more than one tool set

The line is the frame; the **tooling is the profile**. When you move to a second section, what you buy is tooling, not a line.

Three things set the price of a tool set: the number of stations, the roll material and its surface treatment such as hardening or chrome plating, and whether the change system is quick-change or conventional. Say how many profiles you need at the start; every set added later is priced on its own.

## 7. Automation at both ends

The two ends of the line quietly move the price:

- **Entry:** decoiler capacity, loading car, automatic edge guiding, straightening unit.
- **Exit:** automatic stacking, counted bundles, packing and labelling.

Manual stacking is the step that limits line speed and adds damage risk; an automatic stacker removes it but raises the investment. If your line really is to run at 40 m/min, exit automation is not a preference but a requirement.

## 8. Control architecture and brand

The PLC and drive brand, the level of the operator panel, how many recipes it holds, remote access and fault logging. The real question here is not the brand but the **service**: does that brand have a distributor and spare stock in your country? A brand nobody stocks nearby turns a small fault into a week of downtime.

Our lines are controlled with Schneider equipment, chosen for the service network across the markets we export to rather than for the badge.

## Four items a quotation leaves out

1. **Freight and insurance** — a line can be out-of-gauge cargo.
2. **Foundation and services** — concrete, electrical supply and panel, compressed air.
3. **Commissioning and training** — how many people, how many days, in which language.
4. **Critical spare parts** — roll bearings, a servo drive, sensors.

Not being written in the quotation does not make them disappear. It only keeps them out of your budget.

## Putting quotations on the same footing

Six lines have to be defined the same way in every offer. If they are not, what you are comparing is not price but uncertainty:

| Line | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Profile drawing and tolerance | Defines what the line has to make |
| Thickness range and material | Sets the force, and therefore the mechanics |
| Station count | The biggest step in the price |
| The thickness at which speed was measured | "40 m/min" on which sheet |
| Cut-off type and tolerance | Stop-and-cut, flying or die |
| Commissioning, training, spares | In the price or not |

## Conclusion

With a roll forming line the right question is not "how much" but **"what does my profile need"**. Once the drawing, the thickness range, the material, the target speed and the annual quantity are on the table, the price follows, and the offers in front of you become comparable for the first time.
